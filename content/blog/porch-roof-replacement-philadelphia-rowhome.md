# Porch Roof Replacement on a Philadelphia Rowhome: What's Involved

Walk any block in West Philly, Logan, Olney, Wynnefield or the Northeast and you'll see the same thing: a row of front porches, each with its own small roof, many of them patched, sagging or stained underneath. The porch roof is the most-replaced small roof we work on in Philadelphia, and it's also one of the most misunderstood. Homeowners often don't realize it's a separate roof with its own membrane, its own flashing and its own drainage — and that it can leak into the house even when the main roof is fine.

This guide covers how porch roofs fail, what a proper replacement includes step by step, and what to look for on a written estimate.

## Why Porch Roofs Fail

A porch roof has a hard life for its size. It's nearly flat, so water moves slowly. It sits right under the front wall, so every drip off the main roof, the windows and the siding lands on it. And it's attached to the house along one long joint that has to stay watertight for decades.

The failures we see most:

- **The wall joint.** The membrane was stopped short of the wall, or sealed to it with roofing cement instead of being turned up and covered with flashing. Cement dries and cracks within a few years.
- **Old membrane.** Granulated or rubber membrane that's cracked, blistered or worn through. Porch roofs often get skipped when the main roof is replaced, so they can be decades older.
- **Rotted plywood and edges.** Water that gets in sits on the wood. By the time the porch ceiling stains, the plywood near the wall is often soft.
- **No real drainage.** No gutter, or a gutter with no downspout, dumps water on the steps and back against the house.
- **Sagging.** Older porch roofs sometimes have undersized supports. When the beams sag, water ponds in the middle and finds the weakest seam.

## Signs Your Porch Roof Is the Problem

You don't need to climb up to spot most of these:

- Brown stains or peeling paint on the **porch ceiling**, especially along the wall of the house
- A stain or damp patch **inside on the front wall** of the first or second floor, below a window
- Moss, standing water or debris that never dries on the porch roof (visible from a second-floor window)
- The **edge of the porch roof** looks wavy, the metal edge is loose, or the fascia board is soft
- Water pouring off the front edge onto the steps when it rains

If the stain is inside the house on the front wall, people often assume the main roof is leaking. Very often it's the porch roof joint.

## What a Proper Porch Roof Replacement Includes

Here's how the work actually goes on a typical rowhome porch roof. These steps come straight from the way we write our scopes — each one is a numbered line on the estimate so you can see exactly what you're paying for.

### 1. Clear the roof

Anything mounted on the porch roof comes off first: old satellite dishes, antennas, abandoned brackets. Every one of those mounts is a hole in the roof that needs to be dealt with, not roofed around.

### 2. Lift the bottom of the siding or remove the old flashing at the wall

This is the step that separates a real job from a patch. To get the new roof to turn up the wall properly, the bottom row of siding (or the old flashing on a brick wall) has to come off so the new membrane can run up behind it. Then the siding goes back on over it. If a roofer plans to run the new roof up to the wall and seal it with cement, that joint will leak again.

### 3. Tear off the old roof down to the wood

The old membrane, and any older layers under it, come off. On a small roof there's no good reason to roof over old material — you can't inspect wood you can't see.

### 4. Inspect the plywood and replace what's rotted

Porch roofs almost always have some soft wood near the wall or the front edge. We replace damaged plywood and tell you before we do it. If the supports underneath are sagging, they get reinforced with new beams at this stage — it's the only time they're reachable.

### 5. Install the new roof

On a nearly flat porch roof we usually torch down new GAF granulated modified-bitumen membrane, run up the wall and over the edges. On a steeper porch roof, shingles over synthetic underlayment and ice &amp; water shield are an option, and some homeowners choose a metal roof over new ½-inch plywood. The [flat roof materials guide](/blog/epdm-vs-tpo-vs-modified-bitumen-philadelphia) covers the differences.

### 6. New flashing and coping at every edge

New white (or black) aluminum flashing and coping goes around the front and side edges of the porch roof, set in flashing cement, and the wall joint gets covered so water running down the wall lands on top of the roof, not behind it.

### 7. Gutter and downspout

A new aluminum gutter along the front edge with a downspout that carries water away from the steps and the foundation. If the porch already has gutters in good shape, we reattach them; if not, they're part of the scope.

### 8. Put the siding back and clean up

The siding that was lifted goes back on, and every bit of debris goes to the dump, not into your yard.

## Porch Roofs, Bay Windows and the Front Wall Go Together

On a lot of Philadelphia homes, the porch roof doesn't stand alone. Above it there's often a bay window with its own small roof, and the front wall around it may have aging siding or wood trim. They all shed water onto each other.

That's why so many of our front-of-house jobs are written as one scope: porch roof and bay window roof, or porch roof plus new siding on the front wall, or porch roof plus coping around the second-floor windows. When the pieces are done together, every joint between them can be lapped correctly — upper pieces over lower pieces — so water always runs outward. When they're done separately by different people, the joints between them are where leaks start.

If you're getting a porch roof quote, ask whether the bay window roof above it needs work too. It's much cheaper to do it while the crew is already there.

## What Drives the Cost of a Porch Roof

We don't publish prices because every porch is different, but here's what moves the number:

- **Size and shape** — a simple rectangle versus a wraparound porch or one that joins a neighbor's
- **How many old layers** have to come off
- **How much plywood is rotted** and whether the supports need reinforcing
- **The wall** — lifting and resetting vinyl siding is different from cutting new flashing into brick
- **Extras on the same scope** — gutters, downspouts, a bay window roof, siding or window coping
- **Access** — porches over steep steps or tight sidewalks may need scaffolding

A written estimate should name every one of these. If it's a single line that says "replace porch roof," ask what's included.

## What to Look For on the Estimate

Before you sign, make sure the scope spells out:

- **Tear-off down to the wood**, not "install over existing"
- **What happens with rotted plywood** — that it gets replaced, and that you'll be told first
- **How the wall joint is handled** — siding lifted and reset, or flashing cut into brick
- **The membrane or material** going on, by name
- **New edge flashing and coping**
- **Gutter and downspout**, if needed
- **Removal of satellite dishes or old mounts**
- **Debris haul-away**
- **Warranty** in years

## The Bottom Line

A porch roof is a small job with one critical detail: the joint where it meets the house. Get that right — siding lifted, membrane turned up the wall, flashing over the top — and a new porch roof will last as long as the main roof. Skip it, and the porch ceiling will be stained again in a couple of winters.
