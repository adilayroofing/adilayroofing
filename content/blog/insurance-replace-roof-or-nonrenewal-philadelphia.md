# Insurance Says Replace the Roof or Lose Coverage? Your Options in Philadelphia

The letter usually arrives without warning. Your homeowners insurance company has looked at your roof — often from an aerial photo — and decided it needs to be replaced. Do it by a certain date, the letter says, or your policy won't be renewed.

This is happening to more and more Philadelphia homeowners, and it's one of the most stressful questions we're asked. The good news is that you have more options than the letter makes it seem. This guide explains what's going on, how to respond, and when a repair or a written inspection might be enough.

## Why This Is Happening

Insurers have been paying out more for roof-related claims, and many now review the roofs they insure more actively — often with aerial imagery rather than someone on a ladder. They're looking for:

- Missing, curled or patched shingles
- Granule loss and dark streaking
- Visible wear or ponding on flat roofs
- Lifted edges, loose flashing, debris or moss
- Roofs past a certain age or of certain materials

When the pictures show those things, the insurer may require repair or replacement as a condition of renewal.

## Step 1: Read the Letter Carefully

Look for three things:

1. **What exactly they say is wrong.** Is it the whole roof, or a specific condition like a damaged section or a worn porch roof?
2. **What they require.** Replacement? Repairs? Or proof of good condition, like a roofer's inspection report?
3. **The deadline.**

Then call your agent and ask, in plain words: what will you accept as proof the problem is fixed, and by when?

## Step 2: Get a Close-Up Inspection

An aerial photo can't tell a stained but sound roof from a failing one, or a worn porch roof from a worn main roof. A licensed roofer on the roof can. Ask for:

- A **written report** on the roof's condition
- **Photos** of every area the insurer flagged
- A clear statement of what, if anything, needs repair or replacement
- The roof's approximate age and material

Sometimes that report shows the roof has years left and only a specific issue needs fixing. Sometimes it confirms the roof really is done. Either way, you're working from facts.

## Step 3: Know Your Three Paths

### Path A: Repair what was flagged

If the letter names specific problems — missing shingles, a damaged section, a failed porch or back-building roof — a targeted repair plus a written report and photos may satisfy it. Ask your agent to confirm before the work starts.

### Path B: Document that the roof is sound

If the roof is in better shape than the photos suggest, a roofer's written inspection with photos may be enough on its own. Some insurers accept this; some don't. Your agent will know.

### Path C: Replace the roof

If the roof is at the end of its life — widespread wear, multiple leaks, rotted decking — replacement is the honest answer, and arguing with the insurer only burns time. In that case, focus on getting a clear written scope and a timeline that meets the deadline.

## Is It Storm Damage?

If the roof was damaged by a specific event — wind, hail, a fallen limb — that may be a separate, covered claim, and it's worth having a roofer document it. Wear and age generally aren't covered. ## If You Need a New Roof Fast

A deadline from your insurer doesn't leave much room to shop for months. Some things that help:

- **Call early.** Roofing schedules fill up, especially in spring and fall.
- **Get a numbered scope**, not a single price, so you know exactly what's included.
- **Ask about financing.** If the timing is a stretch, [payment plans](/blog/roofing-payment-plans-philadelphia) can spread the cost. We offer financing through Service Finance — see our [financing page](/financing).
- **Ask for the paperwork the insurer will want**: invoice, permit sign-off if required, warranty, and before/after photos.

## The Bottom Line

A replace-or-lose-coverage letter is serious, but it's not always the final word. Read it closely, talk to your agent, and get a licensed roofer to put the roof's real condition in writing. Sometimes a repair or a report is enough. Sometimes the roof really is done. Either way, you'll be making the decision on facts, not on an aerial photo.
