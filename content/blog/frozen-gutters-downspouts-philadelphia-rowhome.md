# Frozen Gutters and Downspouts on Philadelphia Rowhomes: Causes and Fixes

Every January the same calls come in: "There's a block of ice where my downspout should be," "My gutter is pulling off the house," "Water is coming in at the back wall but it hasn't even rained." On a Philadelphia rowhome, frozen gutters and downspouts aren't just an annoyance. When the water can't get off the roof, it finds another way down — usually into the back wall or the top-floor ceiling.

This guide covers why gutters and downspouts freeze, what to do safely when it happens, and the permanent fixes that stop it from happening every winter.

## How Water Leaves a Rowhome Roof

It helps to know how your roof drains, because rowhomes are different from detached houses.

- **Flat main roofs** usually drain toward the back. Water runs to a **drain box** (also called a scupper box or conductor head) at the back edge, which feeds a **downspout** running down the back wall.
- **Porch, bay window and back-building roofs** usually have a small gutter along the edge and their own downspout.
- **Sloped roofs** on twins and Northeast homes have standard gutters along the eaves.

Every one of those is a place where water can stop moving. And once it stops in freezing weather, it freezes.

## Why Gutters and Downspouts Freeze

### 1. Clogs

Leaves, roof granules and debris left in a gutter or drain box hold water. That water freezes, and the ice traps more water behind it. Fall cleaning is the single best prevention.

### 2. Gutters that don't slope to the downspout

A gutter has to pitch slightly toward its downspout. If it sags in the middle or was hung level, water sits in the low spot. Old gutters often sag where hangers have pulled out of soft fascia.

### 3. Downspouts that are too small, crushed or blocked at the bottom

Many rowhome downspouts were undersized from the start, and a lot of them have been crushed at the bottom by trash cans or cars. A narrow or kinked downspout drains slowly, and the bottom section — usually in shade on a north-facing back wall — freezes first. Then the whole column fills with ice.

### 4. Drain boxes that are rusted, clogged or too small

On flat roofs, the drain box is the bottleneck. If the opening from the box into the downspout is small, or the box has rusted and partly collapsed, water backs up on the roof. In winter it freezes in the box, and meltwater has nowhere to go but over the edge of the roof. Rebuilding the drain box with a bigger opening to the downspout is one of the most common fixes we make on rowhome roofs.

### 5. Heat from the house melting snow

On sloped roofs, warm air leaking into the attic melts snow on the upper roof. The water runs down to the cold gutter and refreezes. That's the start of an [ice dam](/blog/ice-dams-philadelphia-prevention-removal).

## What to Do When It's Frozen

**Don't chip or hammer the ice.** It dents gutters, cracks downspout joints and pulls hangers out of the fascia. And a ladder on icy ground is dangerous.

**Watch the inside of the house.** Check the top-floor ceiling and the back wall, especially near the drain box. Stains or drips mean water is backing up.

**If water is coming in, call.** A roofer can clear the immediate backup safely and stop the water.

**If nothing is coming in, let it melt.** Then fix the cause when the weather allows.

## The Permanent Fixes

These are the repairs that show up on our scopes again and again when a rowhome has a winter drainage problem.

### Rebuild the drain box with a bigger opening

On flat roofs this is often the whole answer. The old drain box comes out, a new one goes in with a larger opening into the downspout, and the roof membrane is flashed into it properly. Water leaves the roof faster and has less chance to freeze in place.

### Replace undersized or damaged downspouts

New aluminum downspouts, sized for the roof area they serve, with the bottom carried away from the walk and the foundation. If the bottom keeps getting crushed, it gets a protective boot or a different route.

### Re-hang or replace sagging gutters

Gutters get re-pitched toward the downspout. If the fascia behind them is soft, it gets repaired first — rehanging a gutter on rotted wood just means it pulls off again next winter. Our [soffit and fascia guide](/blog/soffit-fascia-repair-philadelphia) explains why.

### Add a downspout where water has nowhere to go

Porch roofs and back-building roofs without a downspout dump water onto steps and walls, where it freezes. A new gutter and downspout fixes both problems.

### Fix the roof edge

Loose coping or flashing at the roof edge lets meltwater run behind the gutter. New aluminum flashing and coping at the edge keeps water on top of the roof and into the gutter. See [water leaking behind your gutter](/blog/water-leaking-behind-gutter-philadelphia) for how that failure looks.

### Address attic heat on sloped roofs

If ice is forming because the attic is warm, the long-term fix is ventilation and insulation. Ice &amp; water shield along the eaves on the next roof replacement adds protection where ice dams form.

## A Fall Checklist for Rowhome Owners

Before the first hard freeze:

- Clean the gutters, drain boxes and the top of each downspout
- Look for sagging gutter sections and loose hangers
- Check the bottom of each downspout — is it crushed, disconnected or pointed at the house?
- Look at the drain box on the back of the roof from the yard or alley — is it rusted or overflowing when it rains?
- Check the porch and back-building roofs: do they have gutters and downspouts at all?
- Look at the top-floor ceiling and back wall for old stains

If any of those turn up a problem, fall is the time to fix it.

## The Bottom Line

A frozen downspout is a symptom. The cause is water that wasn't moving fast enough: a clogged gutter, a sagging run, a crushed downspout, or a drain box that was too small from day one. Fix the flow and the ice stops being a problem.
