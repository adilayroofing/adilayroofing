# Flat Roof Leaks Every Winter? Why "It's Normal" Is Not the Answer

If your Philadelphia rowhome's flat roof leaks every winter, and a contractor has told you "that's just what flat roofs do," you are getting bad information. A flat roof in good condition should not leak in winter, summer, or any season. The fact that it only happens in winter actually tells us a lot about what's wrong, and almost every cause has a specific, repeatable fix.

## The Four Failure Modes Behind Winter-Only Flat Roof Leaks

In our 20+ years working on Philadelphia flat roofs, license PA184779, almost every winter-only leak traces back to one of these four causes. The fix is different for each one, which is why a contractor who diagnoses by guessing rather than inspecting will keep "repairing" the wrong thing.

### 1. Freeze-Thaw Opening Up Seams

Every flat-roof membrane (EPDM, TPO, modified bitumen, built-up roofing) is assembled from sheets joined at seams. Those seams are the weakest point. In summer, the membrane is warm and flexible, the sealant in the seams is soft, and small gaps can self-seal. In winter, Philadelphia cycles through hundreds of freeze-thaw events from November through March. Each cycle pulls the seam open slightly. After several years of this, the seam opens just enough that a thaw releases water through it.

**How you know that's it:** the leak shows up after a thaw or a winter rain, not after a fresh snow. The water stain inside the house is in a roughly linear pattern, following the line of the seam above. From the roof, the seam looks discolored or has visible separation at the edge.

**The fix:** locate the failed seam, clean it down to clean membrane, and reseal it with the manufacturer-correct adhesive or heat weld. On a small failure, this is $400 to $900. On a roof where multiple seams are failing, plan for a full re-seal or recoat.

### 2. Ponded Water Freezing and Lifting the Membrane

Most Philadelphia rowhome flat roofs are not actually flat. They are sloped at roughly 1/4 inch per foot toward a roof drain or scupper. Over decades, the underlying decking sags, the slope flattens, and water that used to drain now ponds in low spots. In summer, ponded water evaporates. In winter, it freezes. Ice expands roughly 9 percent, and that expansion pries the membrane up off the substrate. When the ice melts, the lifted membrane no longer seals against the deck, and water finds the new opening.

**How you know that's it:** the leak is in roughly the same spot every winter, almost always near a drain or in a known low area. From the roof during a thaw, you can see a clear "ice ring" or staining in a depression. Walking the roof in summer, you can see the membrane has a noticeable bubble or wrinkle at that spot.

**The fix:** drain the low area properly. Sometimes this means tapered insulation added under a new membrane patch to restore slope. Sometimes it means a new internal drain. Sometimes it means a full re-pitch of the roof during a replacement. Patching the visible blister without addressing the slope just buys you one season.

### 3. Ice Damming at the Parapet Wall or Scupper

Most Philadelphia rowhomes have a parapet wall, the short brick wall at the edge of the roof, with a scupper opening to drain water off the front. In winter, snow piles against the parapet, melts during the day from heat escaping through the underside of the membrane, runs to the scupper, refreezes overnight, and builds up. After a few cycles, an ice dam blocks the scupper, the next thaw pools water against the wall, and the water rises high enough to enter the wall flashing.

**How you know that's it:** the leak shows up on the top-floor wall closest to the front parapet, not on the ceiling. You can sometimes see ice or icicles at the scupper or hanging off the parapet during a cold snap.

**The fix:** during the immediate event, clear the ice (carefully, never with a sharp tool, never with rock salt on metal flashing). For a permanent fix, the parapet wall flashing usually needs to be rebuilt with proper through-wall flashing, weeps, and a counter flashing assembly similar to what we describe in our chimney flashing guide. We also assess attic ventilation, since the same warm-attic-cold-eave dynamic that causes ice dams on shingle roofs causes ice dams at parapets on flat roofs.

### 4. A Failed Silver Coat Letting UV Damage the Underlying Membrane

Most older Philadelphia rowhome roofs are modified bitumen or built-up asphalt covered with a reflective aluminum silver coating. The silver coat is the sunscreen for the roof. It is supposed to be recoated every 4 to 7 years. When it isn't, the underlying membrane absorbs UV, dries out, cracks, and starts letting water through at the cracks. Winter accelerates the failure because the dry membrane has no flexibility to handle freeze-thaw movement.

**How you know that's it:** from the roof, the silver coat is chalky, patchy, or completely worn off in high-sun areas. The underlying membrane looks dark, dry, and cracked. The leak pattern is irregular, not following a seam.

**The fix:** if the underlying membrane is still structurally sound, a proper recoat ($2,000 to $5,000 on a typical rowhome) buys you another 5 to 10 years. If the underlying membrane is cracked through, a recoat is just paint over a problem and you need full replacement.

## What "It's Just Normal" Actually Means When a Roofer Says It

When a contractor tells you a winter flat-roof leak is "just normal," one of three things is true.

**Option 1: They actually believe it.** Some contractors learned the trade on a series of failing roofs and never saw a properly built and maintained flat roof. They genuinely think recurring leaks are part of the deal. They are wrong, but they are not lying to you.

**Option 2: They don't know how to diagnose it.** Diagnosing a winter-only leak requires walking the roof during or right after the event, understanding membrane chemistry, and knowing how ice dynamics work at the parapet and drain. A contractor whose entire experience is shingle work doesn't have that toolkit and is telling you "it's normal" to avoid admitting it.

**Option 3: They are stalling you toward an expensive replacement.** Less common but it happens. The contractor knows the repair, knows it would cost you $1,200, and would rather you give up on repair and pay $10,000 for replacement. So they tell you patches don't work on flat roofs.

In any of the three cases, get a second opinion from someone whose primary trade is flat roofing.

## What to Demand on a Diagnostic Visit

A real diagnostic visit on a winter-leaking flat roof should include:

- **A walk on the roof**, with photos of every suspected failure point (we send the photos to you afterward)
- **A check of every seam**, every termination, every flashing, and every drain or scupper
- **A check from inside the top floor or attic**, looking for active water entry and historical stains
- **A written diagnosis**, naming the specific failure mode (seam separation, ponded ice lift, parapet ice dam, UV degradation of the underlying membrane)
- **Repair options ranked**, with prices: stabilization-only, targeted repair, recoat, full replacement
- **Honest assessment of how long each option buys you** before the next problem

If the contractor stays on the ground, points at the parapet from the sidewalk, and gives you a verbal "you need a new roof," walk away.

## Insurance and Flat Roof Winter Leaks

Sudden interior water damage from a flat-roof failure during a winter storm is typically covered under most Pennsylvania homeowners policies as a covered peril. The roof repair itself is sometimes covered (if the proximate cause is a covered event like a windstorm or ice) and sometimes excluded (if it's classified as wear and tear). Read your policy declarations page or call your agent before assuming coverage.

In every case:
- Document the damage with photos and video before any cleanup
- File the claim the same day or the next morning
- Keep all soaked materials until the adjuster has seen them
- Get a written contractor assessment of the cause and the repair

We provide insurance-ready documentation as part of every emergency call. If your roof has been leaking every winter for years, however, the insurance company will likely classify it as deferred maintenance and decline. That is exactly why the right time to fix a winter flat-roof leak is the first winter, not the fifth.
