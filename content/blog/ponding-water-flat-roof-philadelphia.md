## The Industry Standard: 48 Hours

The National Roofing Contractors Association (NRCA), which sets best-practice guidelines for the U.S. roofing industry, defines acceptable drainage as:

> **All ponded water should drain or evaporate within 48 hours of the last rain event.**

Anything beyond 48 hours is considered ponding, which is a defect.

Some manufacturer warranties (Carlisle, Firestone, Versico, GAF EverGuard) define ponding more strictly, often as any water present 24 to 48 hours after rain, and almost all explicitly exclude membrane damage caused by ponded water from coverage.

So if your new flat roof has standing water 72 hours, a week, or weeks after the last rain, the contractor's "that's normal" answer is wrong by every industry standard.

## What Philadelphia Building Code Requires

The Philadelphia Building Code adopts the International Building Code (IBC) and the International Residential Code (IRC) with local amendments. The relevant requirements for flat residential roofs:

- **Minimum slope: 1/4 inch per foot** (a 2% slope) toward roof drains, gutters, scuppers, or downspouts
- **Positive drainage required**: water must flow off the roof, not pool on it
- **Drain capacity**: drain sizing is calculated based on roof area, with primary and secondary (overflow) drainage required on larger flat sections

A roof installed at less than 1/4 inch per foot slope is technically a code violation. Even at code-minimum slope, deck deflection, framing settling, or membrane wrinkles can create local ponding at low spots, which is why proper installation also includes:

- **Tapered insulation** to maintain or improve slope toward drains
- **Crickets** behind chimneys, parapet walls, and HVAC curbs over 24 inches wide to divert water around them
- **Drain bowls properly sized and located** at the lowest points

If your new roof is ponding because the original contractor didn't install tapered insulation, didn't build crickets where needed, or located drains incorrectly, that's a code and workmanship issue, not a "feature" of flat roofs.

## The Three Causes of Ponding on a New Flat Roof

Almost every ponding complaint on a recently-installed Philadelphia flat roof traces to one of these three causes. The fix is different for each.

### Cause 1: Clogged or Undersized Drains

The drains exist but they can't move the water fast enough. This shows up as ponding in a ring around the drain, then spreading outward.

**Common reasons:**
- Leaves, debris, or membrane scraps clogging the drain strainer (especially in fall on rowhomes near mature trees in Mount Airy, Germantown, and Chestnut Hill)
- Drain bowls that weren't reset properly during membrane installation
- Internal drain lines partially blocked by debris pushed in during the install
- Drains undersized for the roof area (more common on older homes where the drainage was designed for a different roof system)

**The fix:** Drain cleaning is the first move ($150 to $400). If the drain is undersized, replacement with a larger primary drain or addition of a secondary overflow drain costs $500 to $1,500. If the drain bowl was set too high during install, lowering it and re-flashing costs $500 to $1,200.

### Cause 2: Local Low Spots from Deck Deflection

The original slope was probably fine but the decking has deflected, either because:
- Original wood plank decking on older rowhomes has compressed unevenly
- Insulation was installed without tapered build-up to compensate for existing deflection
- Decking was replaced in patches during the install, with slight elevation differences
- Trusses or rafters under the deck have sagged over time

**How you know that's it:** ponding is in one or two specific spots, the surrounding roof drains fine, and you can sometimes see the depression when the roof is dry.

**The fix:** Tapered insulation board (polyiso) installed under a patched membrane section to restore positive slope at the low spot. Cost: $1,500 to $4,000 depending on the area affected and which membrane you have. This is usually done as a warranty repair if the install was recent.

### Cause 3: Inadequate Original Slope Across the Whole Roof

The original installer did not build enough slope into the roof. Water ponds across most or all of the surface, drains slowly or not at all.

**How you know that's it:** ponding is widespread, not localized. After heavy rain, the whole roof has visible standing water that takes 5+ days to clear. From inside, the ceiling below shows multiple stain locations rather than one or two.

**The fix:** Full or partial roof re-pitching, almost always done during a complete replacement. Tapered insulation built up across the deck to create positive slope (usually 1/4 to 1/2 inch per foot) toward drains. This is a major job, $8,000 to $20,000+ depending on roof size and complexity, but it permanently solves the ponding problem.

If a recent installation has Cause 3 ponding, the original contractor is on the hook under the workmanship warranty. If the install was 5+ years ago and the original contractor is out of business or refusing to honor the warranty, the cost falls on you.

## How to Document Ponding for a Warranty Claim

If you suspect ponding on a recently-installed roof, document it thoroughly before contacting the installer. The documentation is what supports a warranty claim or a small-claims case if it comes to that.

### Step 1: Wait for a Measurable Rain Event

A documented rain event (1/2 inch or more in 24 hours) creates a clear baseline.

### Step 2: Photograph the Ponded Areas

- Wide shot showing the whole roof
- Close-up shots of each ponded area
- Include something for scale (a ruler, a tape measure, a yardstick laid in the water)
- Timestamp every photo (most phones do this automatically)

### Step 3: Photograph at 24, 48, and 72 Hours After the Rain Stops

The 48-hour mark is the industry threshold. If water is still present at 72 hours, that's clear evidence of ponding.

### Step 4: Measure the Depth

A small piece of dry wood or a ruler laid flat into the water shows the depth. Anything over 1/4 inch is industry-defined ponding.

### Step 5: Write a Dated Log

Date of rain event, depth of ponding observed, photo references, 24/48/72-hour measurements. A simple text document on your phone is enough.

### Step 6: Contact the Installer in Writing

Email, not phone. Attach the photos and the log. Specifically reference NRCA's 48-hour standard and request inspection under the workmanship warranty.

If the installer refuses or stalls, you have the documentation to escalate to a state contractor licensing complaint (Pennsylvania Attorney General, Bureau of Consumer Protection) or small-claims court.

## What "Acceptable" Ponding Actually Looks Like

To be fair, not every wet spot is a warranty defect. Some minor ponding is generally considered acceptable:

- **Less than 1/4 inch deep** at any point
- **Drains or evaporates within 48 hours** of the last rain
- **Doesn't cover more than 5% of the roof surface**
- **Doesn't sit against critical terminations** (parapet walls, drain bowls, vent flashings)

If your "ponding" is a thin film that's gone the day after rain, that's normal. If it's a 1-inch puddle that lasts a week, it's not.

## The Philadelphia-Specific Factors

A few things are particularly relevant on Philly rowhomes.

**Old framing.** Most pre-1950 Philadelphia rowhomes have 2x6 or 2x8 dimensional lumber roof joists or rafters that have settled over decades. The original slope at construction may have been adequate, but after 80+ years of settling, the deck has flat spots that the original installer needed to address with tapered insulation. If the installer used a uniform flat layer of insulation without tapered build-up, ponding is the predictable result.

**Parapet walls.** Most rowhomes have a parapet wall on the front and sometimes rear or sides. Water needs to drain toward an internal drain or out through a scupper in the parapet. If the scupper is set too high, water ponds against the wall. Scupper height is determined at install time and is a workmanship-warranty issue if set incorrectly.

**Adjacent buildings.** Where a rowhome shares a party wall with a neighboring building, the relationship between the two roof systems matters. If the neighbor's roof drains onto yours, your drainage capacity needs to handle both. This is sometimes a surprise to homeowners who think they only need to drain their own roof.

**Tree debris.** Mount Airy, Germantown, Chestnut Hill, and parts of Roxborough have mature tree canopies that drop leaves, twigs, and seed pods onto rowhome flat roofs. Drain clogging from debris is a frequent ponding cause in these neighborhoods and requires homeowner maintenance (or a contracted twice-yearly cleaning).
