# Roof-Over vs. Full Tear-Off in Philadelphia (and What Code Actually Allows)

If you're getting roof estimates in Philadelphia and one contractor is offering a roof-over (installing new shingles directly on top of your existing roof) at a noticeably lower price than the tear-off bids, you have a real decision to make. ## What Philadelphia Code Says

Philadelphia adopts the International Residential Code (IRC) and the International Building Code (IBC) for residential roofing. The relevant rule:

> **A second layer of asphalt shingles may be installed over one existing layer if specific conditions are met. A third layer is not permitted; existing two-layer roofs must be torn off before any new roofing is installed.**

The conditions for an allowed roof-over:

- **Only one existing layer.** If two layers are already in place, a full tear-off is required.
- **Existing layer must be asphalt shingles.** No roof-overs allowed over wood shake, slate, metal, tile, or built-up roofs.
- **Existing shingles must lie flat.** Curled, lifted, or significantly damaged existing shingles must be torn off; you can't roof over a rough substrate.
- **Decking must be sound.** Soft spots, rot, or visible damage in the existing decking disqualify the roof from a roof-over.
- **Roof structure must carry the additional weight.** A single layer of architectural shingles weighs roughly 2.5 pounds per square foot. Adding a second layer adds another 2.5 to 4 pounds. On older Philadelphia homes with original 2x6 rafters, structural assessment may be needed.

The Philadelphia L&I permit application for a roof-over requires confirmation of these conditions, and L&I inspectors do verify before issuing the permit and at final inspection.

So the first question is whether code even allows a roof-over on your roof. On a sizable share of Philadelphia homes, especially older homes that have been re-roofed once or twice already, the answer is no. The contractor offering a "cheap roof-over" on a two-layer roof is either uninformed or planning to skip the permit, both of which are red flags.

## When a Roof-Over Is a Legitimate Choice

A roof-over can make honest sense in a narrow set of circumstances:

- **The existing roof is a single layer of architectural shingles in good visible condition** (no curling, lifted edges, or damaged areas).
- **The roof was installed within the last 15 years**, so the decking is unlikely to have hidden damage from prolonged water exposure.
- **You don't plan to stay in the home long-term.** A roof-over with a 12 to 18 year service life works fine if you'll sell within 10 years.
- **Budget is genuinely the limiting factor right now**, and you understand the long-term tradeoff.
- **The roof structure is rated for the additional weight.** Confirmed during the L&I permit review.

In this specific situation, a roof-over saves real money and the downsides are manageable. We have done legitimate roof-overs for Philadelphia homeowners who fit this profile and were transparent about the tradeoffs.

## When a Roof-Over Is the Wrong Choice

In most situations, a roof-over is a bad value over the long term. The cases where tear-off is the right answer:

### The Decking Has Hidden Damage

On Philadelphia homes 30+ years old, especially those that have had any history of roof leaks, ice dams, or chimney flashing failures, the decking under the shingles has often absorbed some water. A roof-over locks in that damage. When the decking eventually fails, the new shingles fail with it, and the next replacement is a much larger and more expensive job.

We inspect decking on every tear-off we do. On Fishtown, South Philly, and Kensington rowhomes built before 1950, we replace 2 to 6 sheets of decking on the average job. On homes that have had previous roof problems, sometimes 10+ sheets. A roof-over hides all of this. The homeowner pays for new shingles and the decking continues to rot underneath until the new shingles fail too.

### The Existing Shingles Have Any Significant Wear

If the existing shingles are curling, missing granules, cracked, or showing any visible aging, the new layer on top inherits all those problems. The new shingles don't lie flat over curled shingles, water doesn't shed properly, and the new layer fails 5 to 10 years sooner than it would have on a clean deck.

### You Plan to Stay in the Home Long-Term

If you're going to be the owner when the roof-over fails in 12 to 18 years, you'll then pay for a full tear-off of two layers (more expensive than tearing off one layer would have been today) plus a new roof. Total cost: the roof-over now plus the tear-off and full replacement later. Compare that to just doing the full replacement now and being done for 25 to 30 years.

### Manufacturer Warranty Matters to You

GAF, CertainTeed, Owens Corning, and most other major manufacturers limit or void their standard warranty coverage when shingles are installed over an existing layer. The reason: they can't certify the substrate. If the new shingles fail prematurely, the manufacturer will likely deny the claim citing the unwarranted install conditions. A full tear-off keeps the full warranty in place.

### The Roof Already Has Two Layers

Per Philadelphia code, this isn't allowed. A contractor proposing a "third layer install" is either ignoring code or planning to skip the permit. Both are reasons to walk away. We covered the consequences of unpermitted work in our [Philadelphia roofing permits guide](/blog/roof-permit-philadelphia).

## What a Real Tear-Off Includes

A full tear-off and replacement on a Philadelphia residential roof, done right, includes:

- **Complete removal of all existing roofing layers** (shingles, underlayment, drip edge, ridge vents, etc.)
- **Decking inspection** with photos shared with the homeowner
- **Per-sheet decking replacement** for any damaged areas, typically $75 to $150 per 4x8 sheet
- **New ice-and-water shield** at all eaves, valleys, and penetrations
- **Synthetic underlayment** across the entire roof deck
- **New drip edge** at all eaves and rakes
- **New flashing** at chimney, walls, skylights, vents, and any penetrations
- **New ridge vent or other ventilation upgrade** as needed
- **Installation of the new shingle, slate, metal, or membrane**
- **Complete cleanup** with magnetic nail sweep and dumpster removal
- **Philadelphia L&I permit** pulled, posted at the job, and inspected at completion
- **Full manufacturer warranty registration** and a written workmanship warranty

## How to Decide

A simple framework for which one is right for your roof.

**Go with full tear-off if any of these are true:**
- Your roof is 15+ years old
- You have any history of leaks, ice dams, or chimney problems
- Your roof already has two layers (in which case it's the only legal option)
- You plan to keep the home for 10+ more years
- You want the full manufacturer warranty
- You don't want to gamble on what's under the existing shingles

**A roof-over might make sense if all of these are true:**
- The existing roof is a single layer of architectural shingles
- Visible shingle condition is good (no curling, no damage)
- Roof is less than 15 years old
- You plan to sell or move within 7 to 10 years
- You understand the warranty limitations
- Philadelphia L&I will issue the permit (we confirm during inspection)

When in doubt, the answer in Philadelphia is almost always tear-off. The age of the housing stock, the row-home construction patterns, the freeze-thaw weather, and the long-term tradeoffs all favor a clean replacement.
