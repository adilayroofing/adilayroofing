# Torch-Down Roofing on Philadelphia Rowhomes: How It's Installed, Step by Step

If you own a rowhome in Philadelphia, there's a good chance your roof is flat and a good chance the next one will be torch-down. Torch-down modified bitumen is the flat roof we install more than any other — far more than EPDM rubber or TPO. It's not that the other systems are bad. It's that torch-down fits the way Philadelphia rowhome roofs are built: brick parapets, porch and bay window roofs, rear additions, and a neighbor's roof on either side.

This guide explains what torch-down is, why it suits Philly roofs, and exactly how a proper installation goes, step by step, so you can tell whether the quote in front of you covers everything.

## What Torch-Down Actually Is

Modified bitumen is asphalt that's been reinforced with a polyester or fiberglass mat and blended with polymers that make it flexible in cold weather and stable in heat. It comes in rolls. On a torch-down roof, the installer heats the underside of each roll with a propane torch until the asphalt melts, then rolls it onto the roof where it bonds as it cools. The seams between rolls are heated and pressed the same way, so the finished roof acts like one continuous sheet.

The finished layer we install is usually a **granulated** membrane — the top surface is covered in mineral granules, like a shingle. The granules protect the asphalt from sun, add grip underfoot and give the roof its gray or white color. We install GAF modified-bitumen products, and in some spots (like under a roof deck or along certain edges) a smooth-surfaced membrane makes more sense.

## Why It Suits Philadelphia Rowhomes

**It handles the joints.** Rowhome roofs leak at the edges: where the roof meets a parapet, the front and back walls, the porch roof, the bay window, and the neighbor's roof. Torch-down bonds directly to brick, metal and itself, and can be turned up a wall and lapped cleanly. That makes those joints easier to get watertight.

**It's tough underfoot.** People walk on rowhome roofs — to service AC units, clean drains, hang holiday lights, or because there's a deck. A granulated modified-bitumen roof takes foot traffic better than a thin single-ply sheet.

**It handles freeze-thaw.** Philadelphia winters swing above and below freezing over and over. Polymer-modified bitumen stays flexible in the cold, so it doesn't crack as the roof moves.

**Repairs are straightforward.** If a section is ever damaged, new membrane can be torched over it and bonded in. That matters on a roof that might have a satellite dish installed on it ten years from now.

## How a Proper Torch-Down Installation Goes

These steps follow the way we write our own scopes. Each is a separate line on the estimate.

### Step 1: Set up and protect the house

On a three-story rowhome that may mean scaffolding at the front or back. Materials go up, debris comes down, and nothing gets thrown into the yard.

### Step 2: Remove whatever is sitting on the roof

Old satellite dishes, TV antennas and their mounts come off. Every abandoned mount is a hole through the roof. If there's a roof deck, it either comes apart so the roof underneath can be done properly, or the scope says exactly how the roof is handled around it.

### Step 3: Lift the bottom of the siding at the walls

Where the roof meets a wall with vinyl siding — including the neighbor's wall on a stepped roof — the bottom row of siding comes off so the new membrane can run up behind it. It goes back on at the end. This is a step that cheap jobs skip and a big reason why their wall joints leak.

### Step 4: Tear off down to the wood (or prepare for a re-cover)

On most jobs the old roof comes off down to the deck. If there's a single sound layer and the wood is dry, a re-cover can be an option — the scope will say which. You can read more in [roof-over vs tear-off](/blog/roof-over-vs-tear-off-philadelphia).

### Step 5: Inspect the plywood and replace anything rotted

With the roof open, the deck gets checked. Soft or rotted plywood is replaced with new plywood. On older rowhomes this is common near drains, walls and edges. A good contractor tells you what they found before replacing it, and the estimate explains how it's handled.

### Step 6: Torch down the new membrane

The new modified-bitumen membrane is torched down across the roof, with seams overlapped and heat-welded. It's carried up the walls and parapets, around the edges, and tied into any porch, bay window or lower roof that shares the same water path. Where the roof meets the neighbor's, the membrane is lapped so water always runs onto a roof, never between two of them.

### Step 7: Flashing and coping at every edge

New aluminum flashing goes around the roof edges, set in flashing cement. On parapets and roof edges, new aluminum coping (also called capping) covers the top of the wall so water can't get into the brick from above. This is where most flat roofs fail, so it's never optional.

### Step 8: Drains, drain boxes and downspouts

Water has to leave the roof. On many rowhomes that means a drain box at the back edge feeding a downspout. If the old drain box is rusted, clogged or too small, it gets rebuilt with a bigger opening into the downspout. New downspouts and gutters go on where needed.

### Step 9: Put back what was removed and clean up

Siding goes back on, the deck goes back up if it came down, and all debris is hauled to the dump.

## Fire Safety

Torch-down uses an open flame, so it should only be installed by an insured crew that treats fire safety as part of the job: keeping the torch away from exposed wood and openings, using the right base sheet over combustible decks, keeping extinguishers on the roof, and staying to watch the roof after the torch work is done. Ask any contractor how they handle it. If they can't answer clearly, keep looking.

## What Drives the Cost

We don't publish prices because rowhome roofs vary a lot, but these are the things that move the number:

- **Roof size** and how many separate sections (main roof, rear building, porch, bay)
- **Number of old layers** to remove
- **How much plywood** is rotted
- **Edges and walls**: linear feet of coping and flashing, and whether siding has to be lifted
- **Drainage**: new drain boxes, downspouts, gutters
- **Access**: scaffolding on three-story homes or tight alleys
- **Extras**: satellite removal, roof deck take-down and rebuild, chimney work

## What Your Estimate Should Say

- Tear-off down to the deck, or re-cover, stated clearly
- How rotted plywood is handled and that you'll be told first
- The membrane by name (for example, GAF granulated modified bitumen)
- Siding lifted and reset at walls, including the neighbor's side where needed
- New aluminum flashing and coping, with linear footage
- Drain boxes, downspouts and gutters
- Removal of old satellite dishes and mounts
- Debris haul-away
- Permit handling and warranty in years

If those lines aren't there, ask. A flat roof is only as good as its edges.

## The Bottom Line

Torch-down is the workhorse of Philadelphia rowhome roofing because it handles what rowhome roofs throw at it: shared walls, foot traffic, freeze-thaw and lots of edges. The membrane is only half the job, though. The other half is the plywood underneath and the flashing, coping and drainage around it.
