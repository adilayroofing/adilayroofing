## The Four Real Differences

**1. The roof systems themselves.**

Commercial buildings are overwhelmingly low-slope: big, flat or nearly-flat planes covered in membrane systems — EPDM rubber, TPO, modified bitumen, or older built-up (tar-and-gravel) roofing — with engineered drainage through internal drains and scuppers. Steep-slope shingles physically don't work at these pitches; water doesn't shed, it sits.

Residential roofs in most of America are the opposite: steep-slope asphalt shingle, slate, or metal, designed to shed water fast to gutters. Philadelphia complicates this beautifully (next section).

**2. Scale, staging, and logistics.**

A commercial reroof is a project: material cranes or hoists, staged tear-off so the building stays watertight overnight, safety plans, and often work sequenced around an operating business below. Residential jobs are typically one- to three-day efforts with a crew and a dumpster. This logistical layer — not material markup — is much of why commercial projects carry different pricing structures.

**3. Code, engineering, and warranty spec.**

Commercial work runs under the International Building Code with requirements homeowners rarely meet: engineered wind-uplift ratings for the roof assembly, tapered insulation designs that guarantee positive drainage, energy-code insulation minimums, and — for the long manufacturer system warranties commercial owners expect — installation by certified crews following the manufacturer's every detail. The 20-year no-dollar-limit warranty on a commercial TPO roof exists only if the installing contractor is certified and the details are done to spec, with inspections to prove it.

**4. The crews.**

Membrane roofing is its own trade. Heat-welding TPO seams, setting drain assemblies, flashing curbs for rooftop units, terminating membrane at parapets — none of this resembles nailing shingles. A shingle crew improvising on a membrane roof produces exactly the failures Philadelphia rowhome owners know too well: seams that open, drains that leak, corners that peel. When you hire for flat-roof work at any scale, the question isn't "commercial or residential contractor" — it's **"does this crew do membrane work daily?"**

## For Small Commercial Owners: What to Ask For

If you own or manage a small commercial building in Philadelphia — a corner retail block, a small warehouse, a church, a mixed-use property — your quotes should itemize:

1. **Membrane and thickness by name** (60-mil EPDM and bargain thin-mil are different roofs)
2. **Insulation and drainage design** — tapered packages are where flat-roof problems get solved or baked in
3. **Detail scope**: drains, scuppers, curbs and HVAC flashings, edge metal, parapet terminations
4. **Warranty level and what it requires** — a system warranty with certified installation, or a standard material-plus-workmanship pair
5. **Maintenance plan** — twice-yearly inspection with documentation; low-slope roofs reward it and insurers increasingly expect it
