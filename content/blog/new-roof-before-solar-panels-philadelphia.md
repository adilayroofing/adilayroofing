## The Decision Framework: How Old Is the Roof, and What Condition?

The honest answer to "do I need a new roof first" depends on three things: how old the existing roof is, what condition it is in, and how long you plan to keep the panels.

### If Your Roof Is 0 to 5 Years Old and in Good Condition

You probably don't need a new roof. A quality architectural shingle installed in the last 5 years should have 20+ years of remaining life, which matches the panel lifespan. Go straight to solar.

**Caveat:** if the roof was installed by a contractor with a thin reputation, by the previous owner without permits, or with cheap 3-tab shingles instead of architectural, get an inspection first to verify condition. A 3-year-old roof installed badly can already be on borrowed time.

### If Your Roof Is 5 to 10 Years Old and in Good Condition

Get an inspection, but you can usually go to solar. Architectural shingles have a 25 to 30 year service life, so a 7-year-old roof with no curling, no granule loss, no cracked shingles, and sound flashing has roughly 18 to 23 years left. That's a reasonable match for a solar array.

### If Your Roof Is 10 to 15 Years Old (the gray zone)

This is where the decision matters most. A 12-year-old roof in pristine condition might have 15 years left; a 12-year-old roof showing wear might have 5.

**Replace first if:**
- Curling shingles, granule loss in the gutter, cracked or missing shingles anywhere
- Visible sagging, soft spots underfoot when walking the roof
- Existing flashing is rusted, cracked, or has been "repaired" with caulk
- The roof has had two or more leak repairs in the last 3 years
- The shingles are 3-tab (15 to 20 year service life), not architectural

**Probably OK to go straight to solar if:**
- Inspection shows clean architectural shingles with no curling
- Flashing intact, no caulk patches
- Decking sound underfoot, no soft spots
- Attic shows no moisture, no daylight, no mold
- Original installation included full ice-and-water shield and synthetic underlayment

### If Your Roof Is 15+ Years Old

Replace first. Almost no scenario where putting solar on a roof in its last 5 to 10 years of life pencils out. The cost of removing and reinstalling the array when the roof eventually fails will erase whatever you saved by skipping the replacement now.

## The Cost Math: Why Re-Roof First Usually Wins

Here is the real number that drives the decision for most Philadelphia homeowners.

Removing and reinstalling a solar array (a typical 8 to 12 kW residential system, 20 to 30 panels) for a re-roof costs **$1,500 to $5,000**. The work includes:

- Detaching each panel from the racking
- Disconnecting and labeling the DC wiring
- Removing the racking from the roof
- Storing the equipment on-site or off-site
- After the new roof is installed, the same in reverse: reinstall racking, panels, wiring, and re-commission the system
- Often a re-permit through Philadelphia L&I (electrical, structural, or both)

Pricing varies by:
- **Number of panels** ($75 to $150 per panel is typical)
- **Whether the original installer still exists.** If they do, they usually offer "removal and reinstall" as a service. If they don't, you are hiring an independent installer to take responsibility for someone else's mounts and wiring, which costs more and is harder to find.
- **Roof complexity.** Multi-array systems across dormers, valleys, and chimneys cost more to remove and reinstall than a single south-facing array.

So if your 14-year-old roof has 8 years of life left and you put solar on it, you spend $2,500 to $5,000 in year 8 to remove and reinstall the panels when the roof fails. A roof replacement now, before solar, avoids that entirely.

The math gets worse if any of these are also true:
- The roof fails sooner than expected (water damage to the deck, insurance claim, storm)
- The solar installer goes out of business and you have to find a new contractor
- A removal-and-reinstall job damages panels that are no longer covered by their original 25-year warranty

## What Solar Installers Sometimes Don't Tell You

Most reputable Philadelphia-area solar installers will assess your roof and recommend replacement first if it's needed. But a few patterns recur often enough to warrant warning.

### "Your Roof Is Fine for Solar" Without Inspection

If the solar salesperson did not get on the roof, did not look in the attic, and gave you a verbal "your roof is good," you do not have a roof assessment. You have a sales pitch. The solar installer's incentive is to close your solar deal, not to recommend you spend $12,000 on a new roof first.

### Mounting Through Compromised Decking

Solar racking penetrates the roof deck with lag bolts that need to bite into solid rafter or solid plywood decking. If the decking is rotted or weakened, the mounts won't hold and the racking eventually pulls loose. A solar installer who doesn't check the decking before drilling is gambling with your roof.

### Inadequate Flashing at the Mounts

Each lag bolt penetration must be sealed with a flashed mount, not just butyl tape and sealant. A correctly flashed mount uses a metal flashing plate that lifts the shingle above and seals around the lag with a butyl gasket. A poorly flashed mount uses sealant only and starts leaking within 2 to 5 years.

The notorious Sunrun installation that went viral on Reddit, where the installer removed all the shingles directly under each panel, is the extreme version of this same problem: skipping the actual flashing work because the panel "covers it."

### The "We Coordinate With Your Roofer" Promise

Some solar installers will tell you they coordinate with a roofer for any needed work. Sometimes this is real; sometimes it means they call whichever subcontractor is cheap that month. If the roof work matters, hire the roofer directly, complete the roof, and then bring the solar installer in.

## What "Solar-Ready" Means When We Re-Roof First

When a Philadelphia homeowner tells us they're planning solar in the next 12 months, we modify the roof installation in specific ways:

- **Extend ice-and-water shield coverage** beyond standard eaves-and-valleys to include the full array footprint, giving extra waterproofing under future penetrations
- **Use a heavier-grade architectural shingle** rated for the additional uplift and movement that comes with solar mounting
- **Document rafter locations** during decking work, so the solar installer can hit framing on the first try and not have to probe
- **Coordinate flashing details** with the solar contractor, particularly around any conduit penetrations or junction boxes
- **Use a continuous ridge vent compatible with solar arrays**, so attic ventilation is not blocked by the panels

These are minor cost adders ($500 to $1,500 total on a typical project) that save thousands later when the solar goes on.

## Philadelphia-Specific Considerations

A few things matter more on Philly homes than the average national solar guide will mention.

**Rowhome flat roofs.** Solar on a Philadelphia rowhome flat roof is possible but very different from pitched-roof solar. Ballasted racks (weighted, no penetrations) are common and protect the membrane. If the membrane is silver-coated modified bitumen or aging EPDM, get the recoat or replacement done first. We cover the full flat-roof decision in our [flat roof leaks every winter guide](/blog/flat-roof-leaks-every-winter-philadelphia).

**Historic district homes.** Chestnut Hill, Germantown's Tulpehocken Station and Colonial Germantown districts, and parts of Mount Airy have historic district review for any visible exterior changes. Solar on the front (street-facing) slope often requires Philadelphia Historical Commission approval. Solar on a rear-facing slope is usually permitted without review. If your home is in a designated historic district, factor PHC timing into the schedule.

**Permits.** Both the roofing work and the solar work require Philadelphia L&I permits. We pull the roof permit. The solar installer pulls the electrical permit and any structural permit for the mounting. There is also a final inspection by L&I for the solar array.

**Net metering.** PECO offers net metering for residential solar customers under PA Act 213. Your system size is generally capped at 110% of your annual electricity usage, and the credit you get for exported power is the full retail rate (currently). The roof replacement does not affect this, but the solar contractor should be confirming the system size against your last 12 months of PECO bills.
