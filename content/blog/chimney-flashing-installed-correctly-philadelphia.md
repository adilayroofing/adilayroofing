## What "Done Right" Actually Looks Like

A correctly flashed chimney has two separate metal systems working together. Most failed chimney flashing in Philadelphia is the result of a contractor installing only one of them, or sealing both layers together with caulk instead of letting them work as designed.

### 1. Step Flashing Up the Sides

Step flashing is a series of small L-shaped pieces of metal, typically 5 inches tall by 7 inches wide, woven into the shingle courses on each side of the chimney as the roof is shingled. Each piece tucks under the shingle above and laps over the shingle below. Water hits the chimney, runs down onto a piece of step flashing, flows down to the next shingle, and runs off the roof. No water touches the brick.

**What to check from the ground or with binoculars:**
- The metal pieces should be visible as a stair-step pattern climbing the chimney corner
- Each piece should sit flat against the brick, not bent outward
- You should NOT see a continuous strip of metal running all the way up. That's a sign someone used a single L-shaped piece (called "continuous flashing"), which is wrong on any sloped roof

### 2. Counter Flashing Cut Into the Mortar

Counter flashing is a separate piece of metal embedded into a saw-cut in the chimney's mortar joint, then bent down to cover the top edge of the step flashing. The cut is called a reglet. The counter flashing protects the top edge of the step flashing from wind-driven rain and gives the whole assembly a clean, permanent finish.

**What to check:**
- There should be a visible horizontal line on the chimney where the counter flashing enters a mortar joint
- The metal should bend down at roughly 90 degrees, lay over the step flashing, and stop about half an inch above the roof surface
- The reglet (the cut in the brick) should be filled with a polyurethane sealant, NOT silicone caulk and NOT roof cement

### 3. A Cricket on the Uphill Side (Wide Chimneys)

If the chimney is more than 24 inches wide on the uphill side, code requires a small ridge-like structure called a cricket built behind it. The cricket diverts water around the chimney instead of letting it pond against the uphill face. Most older Philadelphia rowhomes have narrow brick chimneys that fall under the 24-inch threshold, but on Mount Airy and Chestnut Hill twins and detached Victorians, the chimneys are often wider and a cricket is mandatory. If yours is wide and there is no cricket, that's a code violation and a near-guaranteed leak source.

## The Cheap Shortcuts to Watch For

Almost every failed chimney flashing job we tear off in Philadelphia shows one or more of these signs. Any of these on a job you just paid for is grounds to call the contractor back.

### 1. A Bead of Caulk or Roof Cement Sealing Everything

The single most common shortcut. The contractor lays a thick line of roof cement (the black tar-like sealant) or polyurethane caulk where the flashing meets the brick, hoping it seals the joint. It does, for about 12 to 36 months. Then the sealant cracks from sun exposure and freeze-thaw cycles, the joint reopens, and the leak comes back. This is the move you see in 80% of "is this acceptable?" Reddit posts. It is not acceptable. Sealant is a supplement to a properly built flashing system, never a replacement for one.

### 2. Surface-Mounted Flashing With No Reglet Cut

The counter flashing is bent over the top edge of the step flashing and just stuck to the face of the chimney with caulk or screws. No saw-cut into the mortar, no reglet. Even if the caulk holds for a while, wind-driven rain in a Philadelphia nor'easter will eventually find its way behind the metal because there is no positive seal to the masonry.

### 3. Single Continuous L-Flashing Up Both Sides

Instead of woven step flashing pieces, the contractor uses one long L-shaped piece of metal running the full height of the chimney. This is fast and cheap but doesn't shed water properly between shingle courses. Water gets behind the flashing and into the deck, often without showing inside the house until significant rot has set in.

### 4. Flashing Nailed Through the Face

You can see nail heads with caulk over them on the visible flashing. Every one of those nail penetrations is a potential leak source. Proper step flashing is held in place by the shingles above, with at most one or two nails in the top corner of each piece, hidden by the next shingle. Counter flashing is held by the reglet cut, not by face nailing.

### 5. The Bottom Apron Is Just a Strip of Roof Cement

The bottom edge of the chimney (the downhill side) needs an apron flashing: a single piece of metal that comes up the face of the chimney about 5 inches and out over the shingles about 4 inches. If you can see roof cement instead of metal at the bottom of the chimney, that's a patch, not a flashing job.

## What You Can Check From Inside the House

Even if you can't climb the roof, you can spot bad flashing from inside. The attic and top-floor ceiling tell the story.

### From the Attic

- **Visible daylight at the chimney chase.** Any visible light around the chimney where it passes through the roof deck means there are gaps that water and snow can also pass through. There should be zero light.
- **Rust streaks down the chimney brick.** Active or historical water staining shows the flashing failed at some point.
- **Damp, dark, or rotting wood** at the framing around the chimney. Soft, discolored, or musty-smelling wood means water has been getting in for a long time.
- **Pinhole rust spots on visible flashing.** If you can see the underside of metal flashing from inside the attic and it has rust spots, it's at end of life.

### From the Top-Floor Ceiling

- **Brown stains on the ceiling or upper walls** near the chimney chase. Even a faint ring is evidence water has been there.
- **Bubbling paint or peeling wallpaper** anywhere on the wall that shares the chimney chase.
- **Visible cracks in the ceiling** running outward from the corner where the chimney passes through.

## What Should Be on the Written Estimate

Before signing, the estimate should spell out every one of these items in writing. If any are missing, ask for them.

- **Material of the new flashing** (copper, lead-coated copper, or 26-gauge painted steel are all acceptable; aluminum is not for chimney work)
- **Step flashing AND counter flashing**, named separately as two operations
- **Reglet cut into the masonry**, not surface-mounted
- **Apron at the bottom**, not just cement
- **Cricket on the uphill side** if the chimney is over 24 inches wide
- **Tear-off of the existing flashing and disposal**, not "installed over existing"
- **Mortar re-pointing** if required, with the chimney mortar joints inspected before work begins
- **Workmanship warranty in years**, not "guaranteed" with no term
- **Permit handling**, if the scope triggers one (most isolated flashing jobs in Philadelphia do not, but if the work includes any structural masonry or significant tear-off, an L&I permit may apply)

## Why Philadelphia Rowhomes Are Especially Vulnerable

Rowhome chimney flashing in Philly is uniquely tough for three reasons.

**Old brick chimneys.** Most rowhomes in Fishtown, South Philly, Kensington, Northern Liberties, and Port Richmond have chimneys that date to the original construction, often 80 to 130 years old. The mortar joints have eroded enough that water can enter the brick itself, not just the flashing. A real chimney repair in these neighborhoods often includes re-pointing the brick along with the new flashing.

**Awkward access.** Rowhome chimneys often sit against a party wall or rear extension. The roofer is working at the edge of a third-story drop with a neighbor's yard below, no place to stage materials. This is exactly the situation where a less-experienced contractor cuts corners and a careful one takes longer and charges more.

**Flat-to-pitched transitions.** Many South Philly and Fishtown rowhomes have a front section with shingles or a low-slope coating and a flat rear with EPDM or modified bitumen. When the chimney sits at or near the transition, the flashing has to integrate with two different roof systems. A contractor experienced in only one of them will get the integration wrong.
